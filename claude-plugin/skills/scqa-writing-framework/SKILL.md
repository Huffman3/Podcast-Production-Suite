# SCQA Writing Framework

## What SCQA Is — and Why It Works

SCQA is a four-part structure originally developed by McKinsey consultant Barbara Minto. It works because it mirrors how human minds naturally process new information: we need context before we can feel tension, tension before we ask a question, and a clear question before an answer lands with weight.

The four parts:

- **Situation** — The stable, agreed-upon starting point. What's the context? What does the reader/listener already know or accept?
- **Complication** — The disruption. Something changes, goes wrong, or becomes uncertain. This is the engine of your narrative — it creates the need to keep reading or listening.
- **Question** — The implicit or explicit question the complication raises in the audience's mind. Sometimes you state it; often it's just the question the audience *feels* without knowing it. It should feel inevitable given the Situation and Complication.
- **Answer** — Your core insight, resolution, or thesis. Everything that follows in the content should support or elaborate on this answer.

The power of this structure is that each section *creates* the next. A good Situation makes the Complication surprising. A good Complication makes the Question feel urgent. A good Question makes the Answer satisfying.

## How to Apply It

### Step 1: Extract the raw material
Before writing, identify:
- What facts/events/context does the audience need to understand first? (→ Situation)
- What goes wrong, changes, or creates tension? (→ Complication)
- What question does that tension force the audience to ask? (→ Question)
- What is the central insight, revelation, or resolution? (→ Answer)

If the user provides raw notes, research, or a rough draft, excavate these four elements from the material before writing anything.

### Step 2: Choose the right variant

There are three common orderings — pick the one that fits the context:

| Variant | Order | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | S → C → Q → A | Most narrative content; builds tension naturally |
| **Lead with Answer** | A → S → C → Q | News-style or persuasive writing; answer first, justify after |
| **Question-led** | Q → S → C → A | When the question is already top of mind for the audience |

For cold opens and narrative scripts, **Standard** is almost always right.

### Step 3: Write with proportion in mind

- **Situation**: Short. 1–3 sentences. Enough context to orient, not so much that you lose momentum.
- **Complication**: This is the heart. Give it space. Build tension. Don't resolve it too quickly.
- **Question**: Often a single sentence, or even implied. The transition from Complication to Answer.
- **Answer**: As long as the content requires. For a cold open, this might be one punchy line. For a full script, this is your thesis and the rest of the episode.

### Step 4: Check the logic

Read it back and ask:
- Does the Situation feel stable and non-controversial?
- Does the Complication genuinely disrupt that stability?
- Does the Question feel *inevitable* — like the audience couldn't help but wonder it?
- Does the Answer address the Question directly?

If any link in the chain feels weak or forced, the structure will feel off even if the writing is good. Fix the logic before polishing the prose.

## Output Format

When structuring content with SCQA, label each section clearly (especially in drafts or outlines):

```
**SITUATION**
[text]

**COMPLICATION**
[text]

**QUESTION**
[text — sometimes implicit, in which case note it in brackets]

**ANSWER**
[text]
```

If writing a finished piece (not a labeled outline), integrate the sections smoothly without the headers — but share the labeled version first so the user can see the architecture before the polish.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Situation that's actually a Complication**: If your "Situation" already has tension or conflict in it, it's a Complication. Back up and find the stable world that existed before the tension.
- **Missing the Question**: Jumping from Complication straight to Answer feels abrupt and preachy. The Question — even just implied — is what makes the Answer feel earned.
- **Answer that doesn't match the Question**: If your Complication raises one question but your Answer addresses a different one, the audience will feel unresolved even if they can't say why.
- **Over-stuffing the Situation**: The Situation is setup, not the story. Keep it lean.

## Example (Cold Open)

**Raw material**: A woman disappeared from a small town. No body was found for 30 years. The man everyone suspected was never charged.

**SITUATION**
Bay Village, Ohio, 1989. A safe suburb of Cleveland — the kind of place where kids rode bikes after dark and nobody locked their doors.

**COMPLICATION**
On October 27th, 10-year-old Amy Mihaljevic was abducted after school. Her body was found in a field four months later. Thirty years passed. Thousands of leads. And the man who took her was never caught.

**QUESTION**
[Implied: *Who killed Amy Mihaljevic?*]

**ANSWER**
That's the question this show exists to answer.
